When INEGI published the results of the 2025 National Survey of Victimization and Perception of Public Security (ENVIPE) for Nuevo León, one figure summed up the picture: 27.4% of households in the state had at least one crime victim during 2024. In absolute numbers, that is 508,800 households out of an estimated 1,859,915 in the state (Source: INEGI, ENVIPE 2025, Nuevo León).

Put another way: if you walk down your block and count four houses, it is likely that someone in one of them experienced a crime last year. It is not a distant statistic or a problem of "other neighborhoods." It is the neighbor next door, the coworker, the relative who prefers not to talk much about it.

The same survey also breaks down the victim rate per 100,000 inhabitants aged 18 and over: 24,933 among men and 21,438 among women (Source: INEGI, ENVIPE 2025, Nuevo León). Both figures confirm that victimization in the Monterrey metropolitan area does not differ by gender as much as is often assumed, and that the phenomenon is widespread.

## Who commits these crimes?

This is where ENVIPE offers a data point that helps us think more clearly about solutions, not just the problem. In crimes where the victim was present, in 60.7% of cases the victim said that only one offender took part (Source: INEGI, ENVIPE 2025, Nuevo León). In other words, most incidents are not the work of organized gangs or complex operations: they are acts of opportunity, committed by a single person.

This changes the conversation. An offender who acts alone usually depends on two things: that no one sees them and that no one can identify them afterward. Those are precisely the two conditions that video surveillance is designed to remove.

Another relevant figure: 19.2% of crimes where the victim was present involved some type of physical aggression (Source: INEGI, ENVIPE 2025, Nuevo León). It is not the majority of cases, but it is not a percentage that can be ignored when thinking about the safety of a home or a business.
